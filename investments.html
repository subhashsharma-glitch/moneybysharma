Investing basics

ISAs, pensions, index funds and fees – the rules for 2026/27 in plain English. Investments can fall as well as rise; your capital is at risk.

ISAs

An Individual Savings Account shelters savings and investments from UK Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax. You must be a UK resident aged 18 or over (16 or over for a cash ISA under current rules).

  • Allowance: £20,000 in 2026/27, which can be split across cash, stocks and shares, Innovative Finance and Lifetime ISAs. Unused allowance does not roll over. The government has said the £20,000 limit stays until April 2031.
  • Stocks and shares ISA: holds funds, shares, investment trusts, bonds and gilts. Values go up and down.
  • Lifetime ISA (LISA): open aged 18–39 and pay in up to £4,000 a year (counts towards the £20,000) until age 50. The government adds a 25% bonus (up to £1,000 a year). You can withdraw without the 25% charge to buy a first home costing £450,000 or less (bought at least 12 months after your first payment, using a conveyancer or solicitor) or from age 60; other withdrawals face a 25% charge, which means you can get back less than you paid in.
  • Junior ISA: £9,000 a year for under-18s.
  • Since 6 April 2024 you can pay into more than one ISA of the same type in a tax year, as long as you stay within the overall limit.
From 6 April 2027: under-65s will be able to put at most £12,000 of the £20,000 into cash ISAs, and interest on cash held inside a stocks and shares ISA will face a charge. The November 2025 Budget also said the government would consult on a new, simpler ISA product for first-time buyers to replace the Lifetime ISA. That change isn't confirmed in law yet, so check GOV.UK for the latest.

SIPPs and pension tax relief

See also our Pensions guide for the State Pension, workplace pensions and a start-age calculator. A Self-Invested Personal Pension is a personal pension where you choose the investments.

  • Tax relief: personal contributions get relief at your marginal rate. With "relief at source", you pay £80 and the provider claims £20 basic-rate relief from HMRC, so £100 goes in. Higher- and additional-rate taxpayers claim the extra through Self Assessment.
  • Limits: relief applies to contributions up to 100% of your UK relevant earnings, or £3,600 gross if you have little or no earnings. The annual allowance (all contributions, including employer) is £60,000, tapered for very high incomes. Once you've flexibly accessed a pension, the Money Purchase Annual Allowance of £10,000 applies.
  • Access age: the normal minimum pension age is 55, rising to 57 on 6 April 2028 (some people have a protected pension age).
  • Tax-free cash: usually up to 25% of your pension, capped by the Lump Sum Allowance of £268,275. The rest is taxed as income when you take it.

The November 2025 Budget announced that salary-sacrifice pension contributions above £2,000 a year will attract National Insurance from April 2029. This affects workplace schemes, not the income-tax relief described above.

Risk, spreading and protection

Investing means accepting that your money can fall in value. MoneyHelper describes it this way: the value can go up or down, but there's a chance of higher rewards than from saving. Whether it suits you depends on your goals, how long you can leave the money and how much loss you could cope with.

How long you can leave it

  • Short-term goals (within five years): MoneyHelper says the general rule is to save in cash deposits, because markets can be down just when you need the money.
  • Medium-term (five to ten years): cash can sometimes be the answer; it depends on how much risk you're willing to take.
  • Long-term (ten years or more): investing may be worth considering, because inflation can seriously erode cash savings over the long run.

MoneyHelper also suggests having three to six months' essential outgoings in an instant-access account first (see our emergency fund guide), and that you think about investing money you don't need for five years. It adds that the only time you shouldn't save or invest is if you need to get debts under control first.

Sources (checked 17 October 2026): MoneyHelper – What's the difference between saving and investing? (opens in new tab); MoneyHelper – Emergency savings: how much is enough? (opens in new tab).

This is a general picture. Risk depends on the specific investment, and the order can differ within each type.

Spreading your money (diversification)

MoneyHelper says you can lower the level of risk you take by spreading your money across different types of investment, which is called diversification. A fund that holds many companies already does some of this for you; our index funds section explains more.

What protection do you have?

  • Investments can fall. The FSCS does not protect you against that. It says it can't accept claims for poor investment performance.
  • If an FCA- or PRA-authorised investment firm fails, the FSCS may pay compensation of up to £85,000 per eligible person, per firm (for firms that failed after 1 April 2019), for example if there is a shortfall in the money or assets the firm held for you. The service or product must be regulated for the cover to apply.
  • This is separate from the £120,000 deposit limit, which covers cash in banks and building societies.
  • Cryptoassets: the FSCS said (May 2023) that the FCA does not regulate most cryptoassets, so it can't protect you if a platform that holds them fails. UK crypto rules are changing, so check the FCA's current page.
  • Check any firm on the FCA Register (opens in new tab) before you invest, and see our scams guide for investment scam warning signs.

Index funds

An index (tracker) fund aims to match a market index, such as the FTSE All-Share or a global index, rather than trying to beat it. They usually have lower ongoing charges than actively managed funds. The value still rises and falls with the market, and the fund's return will be slightly below the index after charges. Things to compare: the ongoing charges figure (OCF), how closely it tracks its index ("tracking difference"), whether income is paid out or reinvested ("inc" or "acc" units), and whether it's a fund (OEIC/unit trust) or an exchange-traded fund (ETF).

Platform fees explained

Your total cost is usually the platform fee (for holding your account) + the fund's ongoing charge + any dealing charges. Platforms usually charge either:

  • a percentage of your pot each year (often tiered, sometimes capped), which suits smaller pots, or
  • a flat fee in pounds, which costs relatively less as your pot grows.

We don't list specific platform prices here until we've verified and sourced them. Check each platform's current charges page.
