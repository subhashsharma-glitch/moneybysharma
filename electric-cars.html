Electric cars, explained

Running costs, tax rules and where things are heading – facts and example figures, with no brands recommended.

Running costs: home vs public charging

Cost per mile = electricity price per kWh ÷ miles per kWh. Charging at home on a cheap overnight time-of-use rate is usually far cheaper per mile than public rapid charging. Prices vary widely, so the calculator uses example rates you should replace with your own.

Vehicle tax (VED)

  • Since 1 April 2025, electric cars pay vehicle tax. For 2026/27, a zero-emission car registered from April 2017 pays the standard rate of £200 a year, and new zero-emission cars pay a £10 first-year rate.
  • Expensive car supplement: an extra £440 a year in years 2 to 6 for cars with a list price over £40,000. From April 2026 the threshold for zero-emission cars is £50,000.
Proposed – not yet law: pay-per-mile eVED. At the November 2025 Budget the government announced Electric Vehicle Excise Duty: 3p per mile for electric cars and 1.5p per mile for plug-in hybrids from 1 April 2028, rising with CPI from 2029/30. Draft legislation for Finance Bill 2026-27 was published on 13 July 2026. It's not law yet, and the details could change.

Company cars and salary sacrifice

Company cars are taxed as a benefit in kind (BIK): a percentage of the list price is added to your taxable income. Zero-emission rates are:

Through salary sacrifice, you give up some gross salary in exchange for a car. Because cars with emissions of 75g/km or less are excluded from the less favourable salary-sacrifice tax rules, you pay BIK at these low rates instead of tax on the salary given up. Sacrificing salary can affect other things linked to your pay, such as mortgage affordability, some benefits and pension contributions. Example: a car with a £40,000 list price at 4% gives a £1,600 taxable benefit in 2026/27, costing a basic-rate taxpayer £320 and a higher-rate taxpayer £640 in tax for the year.

Battery health basics

  • All lithium-ion batteries slowly lose capacity with age and use (degradation), which reduces range.
  • Frequent rapid charging, very high or low states of charge left for long periods, and extreme heat tend to speed it up. Many owners routinely charge to around 80% for daily use, as manufacturers often suggest.
  • Check the manufacturer's battery warranty (length, mileage and the capacity threshold it guarantees). On a used car, ask for a battery state-of-health report.

Why the future points to solar, storage and EVs

Put the pieces together – rooftop solar, a home battery and an EV – and a household can generate some of its own electricity by day, store it, top up on cheap overnight rates, and use far less at expensive peak times. An EV's battery is usually much larger than a home battery.

Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) and vehicle-to-home (V2H) let a compatible car and charger send power back to your home or the grid at peak times, with you paid or credited for it. It needs a bidirectional charger, a compatible car and a suitable tariff, and it's still at an early stage in the UK.

The ZEV mandate timeline

  • 2030: the end of sales of new cars powered only by petrol or diesel, with 80% of each manufacturer's new car sales required to be zero-emission.
  • 2030–2035: new full hybrids and plug-in hybrids can still be sold.
  • 2035: 100% of new cars and vans must be zero-emission.
  • The targets rise year by year in between, with compliance flexibilities confirmed in April 2025. Small-volume manufacturers are exempt from the 2030 phase-out but must meet 2035.

Used petrol, diesel and hybrid cars can still be bought, sold and driven after these dates.
