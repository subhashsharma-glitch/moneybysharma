Home battery costs, VAT and what to check in a quote

A battery’s price is the biggest number in any payback sum. Here is what the Energy Saving Trust says about costs, how VAT on installation is due to change, and what to ask about in a quote. Information, not financial advice.

Try the numbers yourself

Put your own figures into the Home battery payback calculator. The values already in it are examples, so replace them with your own.

Open the home battery payback calculator

Key points

  • The Energy Saving Trust says costs range from £1,500 to £10,000, with a 5 kWh system around £4,600.
  • Installing a battery in a home is currently zero-rated for VAT. From 1 April 2027 the installation reverts to the reduced rate of 5%, according to HMRC’s VAT notice.
  • Compare quotes on the same basis: usable capacity, warranty, what is included and whether VAT is in the price.

What home batteries cost

The Energy Saving Trust says the cost depends on the type and size of battery. It can range from £1,500 to £10,000, and a 5 kWh battery system is around £4,600. It describes a typical home system as around 10 kWh, and the typical lifespan as about 10 to 12 years. It adds that installing a battery at the same time as solar panels, or a heat pump, may reduce the cost, because the installers are already on site.

The Energy Saving Trust recommends getting at least three quotes from installers certified with MCS (the Microgeneration Certification Scheme).

The calculator’s starting cost, £4,600 for a 5 kWh battery, is this Energy Saving Trust figure, and its capacity starts at 5 kWh to match. It is a guide, not a price you will necessarily be quoted, so use a real quote instead.

VAT on batteries

HMRC’s VAT Notice 708/6 explains the temporary zero rate for energy-saving materials:

  • From 1 February 2024, electrical storage batteries installed in residential accommodation qualify for the zero rate. The notice lists a battery fitted with solar panels or other microgeneration, a standalone battery that stores electricity from the grid, and one that stores both.
  • The zero rate applies to supplies up to 31 March 2027. From 1 April 2027, supplies of installations of energy-saving materials revert to the reduced rate of 5%.

The rule is about the installation, so ask the installer how VAT is shown on the quote and on which date the work is supplied.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Assumptions – an example, not a quote. Suppose an installer quotes £4,600 for a 5 kWh battery with no VAT charged, because the zero rate applies. If the same work were supplied on or after 1 April 2027 at 5% VAT, it would add 5% of £4,600, which is £230, making £4,830.

Put those into the calculator with its other starting values (5 kWh, 90% efficiency, 7p cheap rate, 26.32p rate avoided, 300 cycles, 12 years, 2% capacity loss). The first-year saving stays at about £278, but simple payback moves from about 16.5 years to about 17.4 years, and the net result over 12 years from about −£1,606 to about −£1,836. This only illustrates how a price change moves the result.

Capacity for the money matters too. Suppose, purely as a made-up illustration, that £4,600 bought a 10 kWh battery instead. With the other starting values unchanged, the calculator gives a first-year saving of about £556 and a payback with fading capacity of about 8.9 years. The Energy Saving Trust describes a typical home system as around 10 kWh but says prices run from £1,500 to £10,000, so ask each installer for the usable capacity and the total price, then work out the cost per kWh.

Checking a quote

Quotes can look similar and differ a lot. These are questions to ask, not a ranking of any product or installer:

  1. Capacity: is the figure usable capacity or total capacity? The Energy Saving Trust notes the system stops discharging at a set level, maybe around 20%.
  2. Efficiency: what round-trip efficiency is stated?
  3. Warranty: how long does it last, and does it guarantee a minimum capacity after a number of years or cycles? The Energy Saving Trust says to ask about warranties and guarantees for both the battery and the installation.
  4. What is included: installation, inverter, any wiring changes, and any smart or tariff integration.
  5. VAT: is it included, at what rate, and what changes after 31 March 2027?
  6. Tariff and solar: is the battery compatible with the tariff you plan to use, and does it affect solar export payments? See time-of-use tariffs and home batteries and the Smart Export Guarantee and home batteries.
  7. Installer: is the installer MCS-certified, and can they show how they calculated your saving?

Installers may also have a commercial interest in selling a battery. That is a reason to ask how their savings figure was worked out, and to compare it with the sums in the payback article.

Using the calculator with a real quote

In the home battery payback calculator, replace the installed cost, usable capacity, efficiency and lifespan with the figures from your quote, and the cheap and peak rates with those of the tariff you would use. The result is only as reliable as those inputs.
