Credit scores & debt help

What's on your credit file, how to check it free and put mistakes right, and where to find free, confidential help if debts are getting on top of you. Information only – not advice.

Information only – not advice. MoneyBySharma isn't a debt adviser and isn't authorised by the FCA. This page explains how things work; it doesn't recommend any option, firm or product, and it has no affiliate links. For advice on your own situation, speak to a free debt adviser.
Worried about debt right now? Free, confidential help is available from MoneyHelper's debt advice locator (opens in new tab), StepChange (opens in new tab), National Debtline (opens in new tab) (0808 808 4000) and Citizens Advice (opens in new tab). You don't need to pay for debt advice.

Credit files and credit scores

A credit file (or credit report) is a record of how you've managed credit and some bills: loans, cards, overdrafts, mobile and utility accounts, plus your past addresses and public records such as county court judgments (CCJs) and insolvencies. Lenders look at it when you apply to borrow.

A credit score is a number an agency or lender works out from that information. Each agency uses its own scale, and lenders use their own scoring too, so there's no single "credit score".

The three main consumer credit reference agencies (CRAs) are Experian (opens in new tab), Equifax (opens in new tab) and TransUnion (opens in new tab). They are licensed by the FCA. Lenders don't have to report to all of them, so your files can differ. MoneyHelper also lists a fourth agency, Crediva.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): ICO – Credit: your information and credit reference agencies (opens in new tab); MoneyHelper – How to improve your credit score (opens in new tab).

Check your file free

  • Your statutory right. You can ask any CRA for a copy of the information it holds about your financial standing, free of charge. Look for the words "statutory report" on each agency's website, or ask by letter or phone.
  • Timing. Unless it needs more information from you (such as proof of address), the agency has one month to respond. It can take up to two extra months in some cases, but must tell you why within the first month.
  • No subscription needed. The agencies also sell paid monthly services. You don't have to sign up to one to see your file.
  • Which agency? You could start with one report and ask your lenders which agencies they use.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): ICO – Credit: your information and credit reference agencies (opens in new tab).

What affects your file

  • Payment history. Paying on time builds a positive record. Missed payments, arrears and defaults are recorded. The most recent information tends to matter most to lenders.
  • How much credit you use. Your balances and credit limits appear on your file. Staying within agreed limits, including any overdraft, helps show you're managing credit.
  • Electoral roll. Being registered to vote at your current address helps agencies confirm who you are. Councils send updates monthly, so a change can show within about eight weeks.
  • Hard and soft searches. A soft search (eligibility checkers, some quotes) doesn't affect your score and lenders can't see it. A hard search happens when you apply; it shows on your file, and several in a short time can make lenders more cautious.
  • Financial associates. A joint account or joint mortgage links your file to the other person's, and lenders can look at both. You shouldn't be linked just because you live at the same address. When a joint account closes, you can ask each agency for a notice of disassociation.
  • How long records last. Negative information such as missed payments and defaults usually stays for 6 years. A CCJ stays on the Register of Judgments, Orders and Fines for 6 years; if you pay it in full within one month you can get it removed, and if you pay later it stays but shows as paid. A DRO, IVA or bankruptcy stays on your credit record for 6 years.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): MoneyHelper – How to improve your credit score (opens in new tab); ICO – Credit: your information and credit reference agencies (opens in new tab); GOV.UK – CCJs and your credit rating (opens in new tab); GOV.UK – Options for dealing with your debts: Debt Relief Orders (opens in new tab).

Fixing mistakes

  1. Tell the agency. Report the error to the CRA whose file shows it. It checks with the organisation that supplied the information. MoneyHelper says this shouldn't take longer than 28 days, and the entry is marked "disputed" meanwhile.
  2. Go to the source. An entry with a company's name on it is usually that company's responsibility, so you may need to contact the lender too. Lenders can correct entries themselves.
  3. Complain to the ICO. If an obvious inaccuracy isn't corrected by the agency or lender, you can complain to the Information Commissioner's Office (opens in new tab). The ICO doesn't settle financial disputes.

Notice of correction. Accurate information can't be removed early, but you can add a short statement explaining the circumstances (for example, a period of illness or job loss). You ask each agency separately. It doesn't change your score, but lenders must read it, so applications may take a little longer.

If you think someone has applied for credit in your name, report it to the lender and to Report Fraud (opens in new tab) (Police Scotland on 101 in Scotland).

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): MoneyHelper – How to improve your credit score (opens in new tab); ICO – Credit: your information and credit reference agencies (opens in new tab).

Building a credit record

MoneyHelper describes these general steps. They aren't a recommendation to borrow.

  • Register to vote at your current address.
  • Run a current account and stay within any agreed overdraft.
  • Pay bills and any credit on time – Direct Debits can help.
  • Check eligibility with a soft search before applying, to avoid unnecessary hard searches.
  • Be careful about joint accounts with someone who has a poor credit history.
  • Avoid paid "credit repair" firms: they mostly tell you how to do things you can do yourself free.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): MoneyHelper – How to improve your credit score (opens in new tab).

What borrowing costs: the ladder, APR, payday loans and BNPL

Not all borrowing costs the same, and the price you're offered depends on your credit record. This is a rough order of cost, not a ranking of products: always compare the APR and the total you'd repay, and check the lender is authorised on the FCA Register (opens in new tab).

1. Credit union or a 0% dealA member-owned lender, or a card or loan with no interest for a set period.Often lower-cost if you repay on schedule – but the rate you get depends on your record, and a 0% deal usually ends. Check the rate after it ends.
2. Personal loanA fixed amount, repaid in fixed payments over a set time.You know the total you'll repay up front. The rate on offer is personal to you (see APR below).
3. Credit cardA revolving limit you can spend up to.No interest on purchases if you clear the balance in full by the due date; costly if you don't. See our credit cards guide.

6. Loan sharkAn illegal lender.Not regulated, and you can report them anonymously. See below.

Sources (checked 17 October 2026): FCA – Changes to overdraft charges (from April 2020) (opens in new tab); FCA Handbook – CONC 5C.2: charges for overdrafts to be interest rates (opens in new tab).

APR and "representative APR"

APR (annual percentage rate) is a yearly rate of the cost of borrowing, including interest and certain compulsory charges, so you can compare credit. A representative APR is the rate a lender must reasonably expect to give to at least 51% of the people who are accepted for that product after seeing the advert. That means up to 49% can be offered a higher rate. Your own APR depends on your credit record and circumstances, and the lender must tell you before you sign.

Sources (checked 17 October 2026): FCA Handbook glossary – representative APR (opens in new tab).

Payday loans and the price cap

Payday loans are called high-cost short-term credit. FCA rules in force since 2 January 2015 cap what a lender can charge:

  • 0.8% a day of the amount borrowed, for interest and fees combined.
  • £15 maximum in total for default (late-payment) charges.
  • 100% total cost cap: interest and fees together can never exceed the amount you borrowed, so £100 borrowed can't cost more than £100 in charges (you'd repay at most £200).

Illustration: at the cap, borrowing £100 for 30 days could cost up to £24 in interest and fees (£100 × 0.8% × 30 days) – before any default charges. That is the most the cap allows, not a typical or recommended price. In September 2026 the FCA decided to keep the cap at its current level after its 2026 review.

Sources (checked 17 October 2026): FCA Handbook – CONC 5A: cost cap for high-cost short-term credit (opens in new tab); FCA – High-cost short-term credit (price cap kept after 2026 review) (opens in new tab).

Buy now, pay later (BNPL)

The FCA began regulating interest-free deferred payment credit (the most common form of BNPL: repayable in 12 or fewer instalments over 12 months or less) on 15 July 2026. It is regulated when the lender and the seller are different businesses; a plan provided by the retailer itself is not, and agreements taken out before 15 July 2026 stay unregulated.

  • Lenders must be authorised by the FCA, or be in its temporary permissions regime, and must check you can afford to repay before you take out an agreement.
  • They must give you key information first, including repayment dates, amounts and any late fee, and must contact you if you miss a payment and offer support if you're struggling.
  • You can complain to the lender, then to the Financial Ombudsman Service. For agreements covered by the new rules, Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act now applies, the same protection as a credit card (see ways to pay and your protections).

BNPL is still borrowing: missed payments can have consequences, and the FCA notes there are two types – one that charges interest and one that doesn't.

Sources (checked 17 October 2026): FCA – Buy Now Pay Later (opens in new tab); MoneyHelper – Section 75 and chargeback protection (opens in new tab).

If borrowing is getting on top of you, jump to free debt help, priority debts and Breathing Space.

Free debt help

Free, confidential and independent debt advice is available across the UK. Organisations listed on GOV.UK include:

  • MoneyHelper (opens in new tab) – the government-backed service, with a debt advice locator.
  • StepChange Debt Charity (opens in new tab) – 0800 138 1111.
  • National Debtline (opens in new tab) (England and Wales, run by the Money Advice Trust) – 0808 808 4000.
  • Citizens Advice (opens in new tab) – 0800 144 8848 (England), 0800 702 2020 (Wales).

Phone numbers as listed on GOV.UK; check opening hours there. The process differs in Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Be wary of debt adverts and fee-charging firms. The FCA warns that some online and social media adverts make misleading claims about writing off debt or being "government backed", and are run by "lead generators" paid to pass your details on. Fees for an IVA can come to several thousand pounds. Warning signs include pressure to decide quickly and not being told about free alternatives. You can check a firm on the FCA Register (opens in new tab).

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): GOV.UK – Get free debt advice (opens in new tab); FCA – Unauthorised or unsuitable debt advice (opens in new tab); FCA – Debt advice warning: spot the red flags (opens in new tab).

Priority and non-priority debts

Some debts have more serious consequences if they're not paid, whatever their size.

Priority debts (examples)

  • Mortgage, rent and loans secured on your home
  • Council Tax
  • Gas and electricity
  • Court fines
  • Income Tax, National Insurance and VAT
  • TV Licence and child maintenance
  • Essential hire purchase

Not paying can lead to losing your home, disconnection, bailiffs, court action or, in the most serious cases, prison.

Non-priority debts (examples)

  • Credit and store cards
  • Overdrafts and personal loans
  • Payday loans
  • Catalogue and home credit
  • Money owed to friends or family
  • Water bills (but worth treating as essential)

Creditors can still go to court or use bailiffs eventually, but the consequences are usually less immediate.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): MoneyHelper – How to prioritise your debts (opens in new tab).

Breathing Space (England and Wales)

  • Standard breathing space: legal protection from creditors for up to 60 days. Most enforcement action and contact from creditors is paused, and most interest and charges are frozen.
  • Mental health crisis breathing space: for someone receiving mental health crisis treatment. It lasts as long as the treatment, plus 30 days.
  • How to get one: only through an FCA-authorised debt adviser or a local authority debt advice service. You can't apply on your own.
  • Limits: you can't have a standard breathing space if you've had one in the last 12 months, or if you're in a DRO, IVA or bankruptcy. It isn't a payment holiday: you should keep paying if you can, including ongoing bills like rent, mortgage and energy.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): Insolvency Service (GOV.UK) – Debt Respite Scheme (Breathing Space) guidance (opens in new tab).

Debt and mental health

Debt and mental health problems often go together. The Money Advice Trust, which runs National Debtline, works with Mental Health UK's Mental Health and Money Advice (opens in new tab) service to offer extra casework support to people who need it. The Trust also hosts the Debt and Mental Health Evidence Form, which a health or social care professional can complete so creditors can take your situation into account. GPs can't charge for completing it.

If you're in crisis, contact your GP, contact NHS 111, or contact Samaritans (opens in new tab).

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): Money Advice Trust – Mental Health Awareness Week: accessing support and resources (opens in new tab); Money Advice Trust – Debt and Mental Health Evidence Form (opens in new tab).
