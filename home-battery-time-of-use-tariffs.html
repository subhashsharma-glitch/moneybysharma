Time-of-use tariffs and home batteries: what to check

A home battery that charges on cheap electricity only makes sense on a tariff that has a cheap rate. Here is how those tariffs work and what to look at before you rely on one. Information, not financial advice.

Key points

  • On a standard tariff, electricity costs the same at any time of day. A battery needs a tariff with a cheaper period to have anything to save.
  • The size of the gap between the cheap rate and the rate you avoid matters more than the cheap rate on its own.
  • Time-of-use tariffs usually need a smart meter, and you choose whether to change tariff.

Why a battery needs a time-of-use tariff

A time-of-use tariff charges different prices at different times of the day or week. Citizens Advice describes the usual split as a peak (or day) rate and an off-peak (or night) rate, sometimes with more than two rates.

Smart Energy GB points out that on a fixed or standard variable tariff, the price you pay for electricity at night is no different from the price in the day. So a battery that fills overnight on a standard tariff saves nothing. The saving only exists where the tariff has a cheaper window.

The home battery payback calculator assumes exactly this set-up: it charges at a cheap rate and discharges when you would otherwise pay a higher one. The payback article shows the sums.

Static and dynamic tariffs

Citizens Advice splits time-of-use tariffs into two types, and says to check the terms to see which you are looking at:

  • Static: fixed prices for peak and off-peak rates, at fixed times. Economy 7 and most EV charging tariffs are examples, according to Smart Energy GB.
  • Dynamic: the rates change based on wholesale market prices, so they can change from day to day. Some rates can even be negative, which means you could be paid to use electricity at those times. Citizens Advice says you may not get much notice, and that the supplier’s own price limit on these tariffs may be higher than the Ofgem price cap, or there may be none.

A static tariff is easier to plan around. A dynamic tariff can offer a wider gap on some days, but your payback becomes harder to predict.

Smart meters

Most time-of-use tariffs need a smart meter, because it sends readings every half hour so your supplier can charge the right rate for each period. Citizens Advice adds that if you have a two-rate (dual-rate) meter you may be able to use a tariff such as Economy 7 or Economy 10 without a smart meter.

Smart Energy GB also says that having a smart meter does not change your price. You only change tariff if you choose to.

Three things to check

  1. The cheap rate and its hours. How many hours are cheap, and are they when the battery can charge? A short window may not fill a large battery.
  2. The rate you would avoid, and the standing charge. The “peak rate you avoid” in the calculator is your tariff’s peak or day rate, not necessarily the price cap. Ofgem notes that for multi-rate tariffs such as Economy 7, the peak and off-peak rates together cannot be more than the price cap. Rates on other tariffs can differ. Also compare daily standing charges.
  3. The rest of your home. The Energy Saving Trust notes that these tariffs usually apply to all the electricity used in the home. If your other use falls in the pricier hours, you may pay more for that part. The battery can cover some of it, but not necessarily all of it.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Assumptions – an example, not a quote. The same battery as in the payback article: 10 kWh usable, 90% round-trip efficiency, 300 full cycles a year, £4,000 installed, 12-year life, capacity falls 2% a year. The rate avoided is 26.32p/kWh. Only the cheap rate changes, and the three cheap rates (7p, 12p, 20p) are made up for illustration.

The gap shrinks by about two-thirds between the first and last rows, but the saving falls by nearly four-fifths. That is because the energy lost in the battery is bought at the cheap rate too, so a narrow gap leaves very little margin.

The Energy Saving Trust’s August 2026 research modelled over a million combinations of technology and tariff. It found tariff choice mattered a great deal, and that batteries did better paired with a heat pump on a suitable tariff than with solar panels alone. Its figures are scenario-based, so your results will differ.

Contracts and the price cap

  • Fixed tariffs keep your rates for the contract. Citizens Advice says that with 49 days or less left you can switch without an exit fee, but with 50 days or more you might have to pay one.
  • The price cap limits unit rates and standing charges on default tariffs, and does not limit your total bill. Ofgem’s average electricity unit rate rises from 26.32p/kWh (1 October to 31 December 2026) to 32.69p/kWh (1 January to 31 March 2027). These are national Direct Debit averages, and your own rate depends on your region and how you pay.

Questions to ask about a tariff

  1. What are the cheap and peak rates, the hours they apply, and the daily standing charge?
  2. Is it static or dynamic? If dynamic, what is the highest price I could pay?
  3. Do I need a smart meter, and is a battery allowed to charge during the cheap window?
  4. How long is the contract, and is there an exit fee?
  5. What would my other electricity use cost at the peak or day rate?
