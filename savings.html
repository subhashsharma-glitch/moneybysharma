The main types of savings account

Easy access

You can pay in and take money out when you like. The rate is usually variable, so it can go up or down at any time – often following changes to the Bank of England Bank Rate (currently 3.75%). Some accounts limit the number of withdrawals.

Notice accounts

You must give notice (for example 30, 60 or 95 days) before withdrawing. Rates are usually variable.

Fixed-rate bonds

You lock money away for a set term (often 1–5 years) at a fixed rate. You usually cannot add money after opening, and early access is often not allowed or carries a penalty. Interest may be paid monthly, yearly or at maturity – which can affect which tax year it falls in.

Cash ISAs

Interest in an ISA is free of UK Income Tax and doesn't use your Personal Savings Allowance. For 2026/27 the overall ISA allowance is £20,000, which you can put entirely into a cash ISA. Cash ISAs come in easy access, notice and fixed versions. Check whether yours is "flexible" (lets you withdraw and replace money in the same tax year).

Changing from 6 April 2027: following the November 2025 Budget, the annual cash ISA limit falls to £12,000 for people aged 64 or under at the end of the tax year. The overall ISA allowance stays at £20,000, and people aged 65 or over keep a £20,000 cash ISA limit. New rules will also stop transfers from stocks and shares ISAs into cash ISAs for under-65s. The regulations (SI 2026/1018) were made on 10 September 2026. None of this affects the 2026/27 tax year.

Regular savers

You pay in a fixed monthly amount (often £25–£500) for usually 12 months. Headline rates can look high, but because money goes in gradually, the interest you actually earn is roughly half what the AER suggests on the full year-end balance. Many require a current account with the same provider.

FSCS protection: £120,000

Since 1 December 2025, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme protects eligible deposits up to £120,000 per person, per authorised firm (it was £85,000 from 2017 to 30 November 2025). Key points:

  • Brands that share one banking licence count as one firm, so the limit covers your total across them.
  • Joint accounts: each holder is protected up to £120,000.
  • Temporary high balances (for example from a house sale or inheritance) can be protected up to £1.4 million for up to six months.
  • Cash ISAs with UK-authorised banks and building societies are covered.

You can check a firm on the FSCS protection checker.

How savings interest is taxed (2026/27)

Savings tax allowances
Allowance2026/27
Personal Allowance£12,570 (reduced by £1 for every £2 of income over £100,000)
Starting rate for savings0% on up to £5,000 of savings income – reduced by £1 for every £1 of other income above your Personal Allowance
Personal Savings Allowance – basic-rate taxpayer£1,000
Personal Savings Allowance – higher-rate taxpayer£500
Personal Savings Allowance – additional-rate taxpayer£0
Savings tax rates above allowances20% basic, 40% higher, 45% additional

Example: if your only income is a salary of £15,000, your other income is £2,430 above the Personal Allowance, so your starting rate band is £5,000 − £2,430 = £2,570. You could then earn £2,570 of interest at 0% plus £1,000 under the Personal Savings Allowance before paying tax.

Changing from 6 April 2027: savings income tax rates rise by 2 percentage points to 22% / 42% / 47% (Finance Act 2026). The Personal Savings Allowance and starting rate figures above are for 2026/27. Scottish taxpayers: savings income uses UK (not Scottish) rates and bands.
