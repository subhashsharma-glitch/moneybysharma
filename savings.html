The main types of savings account

Easy access

You can pay in and take money out when you like. The rate is usually variable, so it can go up or down at any time – often following changes to the Bank of England Bank Rate (currently 3.75%). Some accounts limit the number of withdrawals.

Notice accounts

You must give notice (for example 30, 60 or 95 days) before withdrawing. Rates are usually variable.

Fixed-rate bonds

You lock money away for a set term (often 1–5 years) at a fixed rate. You usually cannot add money after opening, and early access is often not allowed or carries a penalty. Interest may be paid monthly, yearly or at maturity – which can affect which tax year it falls in.

Cash ISAs

Interest in an ISA is free of UK Income Tax and doesn't use your Personal Savings Allowance. For 2026/27 the overall ISA allowance is £20,000, which you can put entirely into a cash ISA. Cash ISAs come in easy access, notice and fixed versions. Check whether yours is "flexible" (lets you withdraw and replace money in the same tax year).

Cash ISA cut cancelled (Budget, 28 October 2026): the planned cut to the cash ISA limit for under-65s from 6 April 2027 has been scrapped. The government says the cash ISA limit will stay at £20,000 for all savers aged 18 or over from April 2027, and transfers from stocks and shares ISAs into cash ISAs remain allowed. The earlier regulations (SI 2026/1018) are due to be reversed by new regulations to be laid before Parliament in November 2026, so the reversal isn't law yet. Nothing changes for the 2026/27 tax year.

Regular savers

You pay in a fixed monthly amount (often £25–£500) for usually 12 months. Headline rates can look high, but because money goes in gradually, the interest you actually earn is roughly half what the AER suggests on the full year-end balance. Many require a current account with the same provider.

How interest grows: simple, compound and the Rule of 72

Simple interest is paid only on the money you first put in. Compound interest is paid on that money and on the interest already added, so growth builds on itself. Savings accounts normally pay compound interest, and so do many debts.

Simple: £40 a year. Compound: interest added yearly. Worked from the standard formula: £1,000 × 1.04years. After 10 years that is £1,480.24, against £1,400 with simple interest. It ignores tax and assumes the rate never changes, which is unusual for a real account.

AER and how often interest is added

The more often interest is added, the more it earns on itself. The AER (Annual Equivalent Rate) puts every account on the same footing: it is what the rate would be if interest were added once a year. For example, 4% a year paid monthly works out at an AER of about 4.07% ((1 + 0.04 ÷ 12)12 − 1). That is why AER is the figure to compare between accounts.

The Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 is a quick way to estimate how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the yearly rate. It is a mental shortcut, not an exact figure.

  • At 4% a year: 72 ÷ 4 = about 18 years to double.
  • At 8% a year: 72 ÷ 8 = about 9 years to double.

FSCS protection: £120,000

Since 1 December 2025, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme protects eligible deposits up to £120,000 per person, per authorised firm (it was £85,000 from 2017 to 30 November 2025). Key points:

  • Brands that share one banking licence count as one firm, so the limit covers your total across them.
  • Joint accounts: each holder is protected up to £120,000.
  • Temporary high balances (for example from a house sale or inheritance) can be protected up to £1.4 million for up to six months.
  • Cash ISAs with UK-authorised banks and building societies are covered.

You can check a firm on the FSCS protection checker (opens in new tab).

How savings interest is taxed (2026/27)

Savings tax allowances
Allowance2026/27
Personal Allowance£12,570 (reduced by £1 for every £2 of income over £100,000)

Personal Savings Allowance – basic-rate taxpayer£1,000

Savings tax rates above allowances20% basic, 40% higher, 45% additional

Example: if your only income is a salary of £15,000, your other income is £2,430 above the Personal Allowance, so your starting rate band is £5,000 − £2,430 = £2,570. You could then earn £2,570 of interest at 0% plus £1,000 under the Personal Savings Allowance before paying tax.

Changing from 6 April 2027 (subject to Finance Bill): Budget 2026 announced savings income tax rates for 2027/28 of 24% / 44% / 49% (basic / higher / additional). That's 2 percentage points above the 22% / 42% / 47% set by Finance Act 2026. It's being legislated in Finance Bill 2026-27, so it isn't law yet. The Personal Savings Allowance (£1,000 / £500 / £0) and the £5,000 starting rate for savings stay the same for 2027/28. Scottish taxpayers: savings income uses UK (not Scottish) rates and bands.

Common questions

How much can I put in a cash ISA in 2026/27?

The overall ISA allowance for 2026/27 is £20,000, and you can put all of it into a cash ISA. At Budget 2026 (28 October 2026) the planned cut for under-65s from April 2027 was scrapped, so the cash ISA limit is due to stay at £20,000 for savers aged 18 or over. See Budget 2026 for savers.

How much savings interest can I earn before paying tax?

The Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000 for basic-rate taxpayers, £500 for higher-rate and £0 for additional-rate taxpayers. Some people can also use the starting rate for savings of up to £5,000. Interest in an ISA is tax-free and doesn't use your allowance.

How much of my savings is protected if a bank fails?

Since 1 December 2025 the FSCS protects eligible deposits up to £120,000 per person, per authorised firm. Brands that share one banking licence count as one firm. See FSCS protection.

What is AER?

The Annual Equivalent Rate (AER) shows what a rate would be if interest were added once a year, so you can compare accounts fairly. For example, 4% a year paid monthly works out at an AER of about 4.07%.

Are gilts an alternative to a savings account?

Some people compare them. Gains on gilts are free of Capital Gains Tax, while the coupon is taxed as savings income. See Gilts explained and its gilt vs savings calculator.
