The Smart Export Guarantee and home batteries

The Smart Export Guarantee pays you for some electricity you send to the grid. A battery changes what you export and what counts. Here is how it works. Information, not financial advice.

Key points

  • The SEG pays small-scale generators, such as solar panels, for the low-carbon electricity they export. A battery is not itself an eligible generator.
  • Suppliers set their own SEG rates and contract terms, but the rate must be above zero.
  • Under the SEG, a supplier is not obliged to pay for electricity you bought from the grid, stored and exported. Check before you assume it.

What the Smart Export Guarantee is

Ofgem says the SEG launched on 1 January 2020. It requires some electricity suppliers, called SEG licensees, to pay small-scale generators for low-carbon electricity they export back to the National Grid, as long as certain criteria are met. Payments are calculated from export meter readings.

SEG licensees decide the rate, contract length and other terms. Any SEG tariff must always be above zero. Ofgem does not set a rate, and this page does not quote one, because rates differ between suppliers and change over time. The home battery payback calculator does not include SEG payments.

Who can claim

  • Ofgem lists five eligible technology types: solar photovoltaic (solar PV), wind, micro combined heat and power, hydro and anaerobic digestion. The installation must be in Great Britain, meet the eligibility criteria and be within the capacity limits (up to 5MW, or 50kW for micro-CHP).
  • Ofgem’s guidance for licensees says suppliers with at least 150,000 domestic electricity customers must offer at least one SEG tariff. Smaller suppliers can offer one voluntarily.
  • You can apply to any SEG licensee. It does not have to be your current electricity supplier.

If you have solar panels and a battery

With solar, a battery changes what happens to your spare electricity. Without a battery, surplus solar is exported and paid for under a SEG tariff. With a battery, you can store some of that surplus and use it later, which avoids buying from the grid at your normal rate.

Ofgem has published separate guidance for adding electricity storage to installations that receive SEG payments (version 6.2, 23 May 2025). The central question is whether the export meter can tell electricity generated by the eligible installation apart from electricity from other sources. Check with your SEG supplier how it would treat your set-up before you buy.

The Energy Saving Trust’s research (12 August 2026) found that, strictly on savings, adding a battery to solar panels may not be enough to justify the cost, because current export tariff rates are good enough that selling surplus can be almost as valuable as storing it. It adds that a battery can protect you if export rates fall, because stored electricity then becomes more valuable to use yourself.

Batteries charged from the grid

This is where the SEG is often misunderstood. Ofgem’s guidance for licensees describes “green export” as electricity generated by the eligible installation, and says a licensee is only obliged to make SEG payments for that. Where the export meter also records electricity from another source that it cannot separate out (“brown export”), the licensee is not obliged to pay, although it can choose to. One of Ofgem’s examples is co-located storage that is charged from a source other than the eligible generator.

So the idea of buying cheap electricity overnight and exporting it for a payment is not something a supplier has to pay for under the SEG. Some suppliers may offer separate schemes with their own terms. That is a supplier’s commercial choice, so treat any such income as uncertain until it is confirmed in writing. This is why the calculator’s battery saving comes from using the stored energy in your own home, not from exporting it.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Assumptions – an example, not a quote. A home with solar panels and a battery. Spare solar is either exported at an example SEG rate of 15p/kWh (made up) or stored and used later. Stored energy is used in the evening instead of buying electricity at 26.32p/kWh (Ofgem’s average unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026). Round-trip battery efficiency 90%.

  • Export 1 kWh: 15p.
  • Store 1 kWh: you get back 0.90 kWh, which avoids buying 0.90 kWh at 26.32p, so about 23.7p.
  • Difference: about 8.7p per kWh stored, or about £87 for every 1,000 kWh moved into the battery.

On these made-up numbers the advantage is real but modest. It shrinks as the export rate rises. If the example export rate were 25p/kWh, storing would give about 23.7p against 25p exported, a small loss of about 1.3p per kWh. It also depends on how many kWh you actually shift in a year, which depends on your solar output and your evening use.

Questions to ask a supplier or installer

  1. Is this a SEG tariff, and what are the rate, the contract length and how often the rate can change?
  2. Will my battery and metering let the supplier separate solar export from any grid-charged electricity, and will it pay on that basis? Ask for this in writing.
  3. Can the battery be set to charge only from solar, and does that suit my tariff plan?
  4. Is the export meter I need included in the quote?
