EV tariffs and off-peak charging

An EV tariff makes electricity cheaper at certain hours, usually overnight. Whether it saves you money depends on the rest of your home’s use too. Here is how to think it through. Information, not financial advice.

Open the ev charging cost calculator

Key points

  • An EV tariff is a type of time-of-use tariff with a cheaper rate, usually overnight, so you can charge a car at a lower price.
  • Most EV tariffs apply to all the electricity used in the home, so the rest of your use at the higher rate matters.
  • Home charge points sold for private use in Great Britain must have off-peak default charging hours that you can change.

What an EV tariff is

Citizens Advice says you can charge an electric vehicle on a standard variable or fixed tariff, but a time-of-use tariff usually works out cheaper. If you charge at night, a static time-of-use tariff, such as an EV tariff, might be cheaper. If you charge at different times of day, a dynamic tariff might save you money. Smart Energy GB says most EV charging tariffs are static, with fixed rates at fixed times, and that most flexible tariffs need a smart meter.

On a standard tariff, electricity costs the same at any time of day, so charging at night is not cheaper. See time-of-use tariffs and home batteries for more on static and dynamic tariffs, which apply to batteries and cars alike.

Smart charge points and off-peak hours

You do not need a smart charge point to charge off-peak. The Energy Saving Trust says you can plug in manually during low-cost periods, or set a charging time through the car’s dashboard controls.

If you do have a smart one, GOV.UK says charge points sold for private (home or workplace) use in Great Britain must have smart functionality, under regulations in force since 30 June 2022. They must also include pre-set off-peak default charging hours, which the owner can accept, remove or change. The regulations define peak hours as 8am to 11am and 4pm to 10pm on weekdays, and the default hours must be outside those times. The owner must also be able to override the schedule. A charger’s default hours are not the same as your tariff’s cheap hours, so check that the two match.

The Energy Saving Trust says smart charging at home overnight could save up to 20% on charging costs, depending on your tariff, mileage and charging patterns. It gives the typical cost of installing a home charge point as around £800 to £1,200 (page last updated March 2025), and says you will typically need a smart meter and an EV or time-of-use tariff to make the most of it.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Assumptions – an example, not a quote. A household that charges an EV with about 2,286 kWh a year (8,000 miles at 3.5 miles per kWh) and uses a further 2,500 kWh a year on everything else, all at the day rate. Standard tariff: 32.69p/kWh at any time (Ofgem’s average for 1 January to 31 March 2027). EV tariff: a made-up 7p/kWh at night and 40p/kWh by day. All EV charging happens at night. Standing charges and charging losses are ignored.

  • Saving on EV charging: 2,286 kWh × (32.69p − 7p) = about £587.
  • Extra cost on the rest of the home: 2,500 kWh × (40p − 32.69p) = about £183.
  • Net: about £404 a year better off on this made-up tariff, before any difference in standing charge.

If the day rate were 45p rather than 40p, the extra cost on the rest of the home would be about £308 a year, and the net saving would fall to about £279. If it were 30p, the rest of the home would cost less than on the standard tariff and the net saving would be larger. And if less of the car’s charging happened at night than assumed, the saving would be smaller still. The sum to remember is: kWh moved to the cheap rate × the saving per kWh, minus the extra cost on all your other use. The EV charging cost calculator lets you try different home rates.

Whole-home or EV-only

The Energy Saving Trust says that most of these tariffs apply to all the electricity used in the home. Some suppliers offer tariffs where the cheaper rate is for EV charging only and is separate from other use (it calls these type-of-use tariffs). The two work differently, so ask which one you are being offered. Whole-home tariffs also reward shifting other use, such as running a washing machine or dishwasher on a timer, as Smart Energy GB suggests.

Smart Energy GB also says that you choose your tariff. When you get a smart meter the price you pay does not change unless you switch. Not every household suits a time-of-use tariff.

Questions to ask about an EV tariff

  1. What are the night and day rates, the cheap hours, and the standing charge?
  2. Does the cheap rate apply to the whole home or only to the car?
  3. Is it a static or dynamic tariff? If dynamic, what is the highest price I could pay?
  4. Do I need a smart meter and a smart charge point, and will the charger’s hours match the tariff’s?
  5. How long is the contract, and what is the exit fee?
