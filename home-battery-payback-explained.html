How home battery payback is worked out

A payback figure sounds precise, but it is only as good as the five or six numbers behind it. Here are the sums, step by step, and which inputs move the answer most. Information, not financial advice.

Try the numbers yourself

Put your own figures into the Home battery payback calculator. The values already in it are examples, so replace them with your own.

Open the home battery payback calculator

Key points

  • Payback is the battery’s cost divided by what it saves each year. The saving comes from the gap between a cheap rate and the rate you avoid paying.
  • A battery loses some energy each time it is used, and it slowly loses capacity as it ages. Both reduce the saving.
  • In the example below, the battery does not pay back within its 12-year life. It takes a combination of changes to get it there.

The idea

In this calculator, a home battery on a cheap-rate tariff does one job. It charges when electricity is cheap, then supplies your home when electricity would otherwise cost more. The saving is the difference between the two prices, less the energy lost along the way. Whether that adds up to more than the battery’s cost depends on how big the difference is and how often you can use it.

This only works on a tariff with a cheaper rate at some times of day (see time-of-use tariffs and home batteries). On a standard tariff the price is the same at any time, so there is no gap to use. Smart Energy GB says it is only cheaper to use electricity at night if you are on a tariff that charges less at night.

The sums, step by step

These are the same steps the home battery payback calculator uses.

  1. Energy you buy to fill the battery. Usable capacity ÷ round-trip efficiency. Because some energy is lost, you buy a bit more than you get back.
  2. What that charge costs. Energy bought × the cheap rate.
  3. What the same energy is worth when you use it. Usable capacity × the rate you avoid paying.
  4. Saving per cycle. Step 3 minus step 2.
  5. First-year saving. Saving per cycle × full cycles per year.
  6. Later years. The saving falls each year by the capacity loss (degradation) rate, because the battery holds less.
  7. Payback. The point where the running total of savings reaches the installed cost. If it never does within the battery’s lifespan, the calculator says so.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Assumptions – an example, not a quote. 5 kWh usable battery. 90% round-trip efficiency. Cheap rate 7p/kWh (made up). Rate avoided 26.32p/kWh (Ofgem’s average electricity unit rate for 1 October to 31 December 2026, Direct Debit). 300 full cycles a year. Cost £4,600 (the Energy Saving Trust’s figure for a 5 kWh battery system; a real quote will differ). 12-year lifespan. Capacity falls 2% a year (made up). These are the calculator’s starting values.

  • Energy bought: 5 ÷ 0.90 = 5.56 kWh. Cost: 5.56 × 7p = about 39p.
  • Value when used: 5 × 26.32p = about £1.32.
  • Saving per cycle: £1.32 − £0.39 = about £0.93.
  • First-year saving: £0.93 × 300 = about £278.
  • Simple payback ignoring fading capacity: £4,600 ÷ £278 = about 16.5 years. With 2% a year capacity loss, it does not pay back within 12 years.
  • Total saving over 12 years after fading: about £2,994, so a net result of about −£1,606 after the £4,600 cost.

On these assumptions the battery does not pay for itself within its life. That is not a verdict on any particular battery. The next table shows what would change the answer.

The 32.69p rows use Ofgem’s average electricity unit rate for 1 January to 31 March 2027 (announced 25 November 2026). They only show how sensitive the answer is. The price cap changes every three months, and the rate you actually avoid is your own tariff’s peak or day rate, which may be higher or lower than the cap.

Two lessons stand out. The gap between the cheap rate and the rate you avoid matters most. And you only gain from cycles you actually use, so a battery that sits part-empty because your evening demand is low saves less than the formula suggests.

What the sums leave out

  • Standing charges. A tariff with a cheap night rate may have a different daily standing charge from your current one. The calculator ignores this.
  • Changing prices. The sums assume tariffs stay the same for the battery’s whole life. Ofgem’s average electricity unit rate alone moves from 26.32p to 32.69p per kWh between two consecutive quarters.
  • Usable vs headline capacity. The Energy Saving Trust says a system stops taking electricity out of the battery at a set level, maybe around 20% of total capacity. Use the usable figure from the datasheet or quote.
  • Lifespan and replacement. The Energy Saving Trust gives a typical battery lifespan of about 10 to 12 years and says to ask about warranties. The calculator ignores maintenance and inverter replacement.
  • The time value of money. £556 saved in year 10 is worth less to you than £556 today.
  • Solar and export payments. The calculator models grid-charged, cheap-rate use only. If you have solar panels, see the Smart Export Guarantee and home batteries.

Questions to ask before relying on a payback figure

  1. Which tariff is the saving based on, and what are its cheap and peak rates and standing charge?
  2. How many full cycles a year are assumed, and do I have enough evening or peak demand to use them?
  3. Is the capacity quoted usable capacity, and what round-trip efficiency is assumed?
  4. What does the warranty cover, for how long, and does it set a limit on capacity loss?
  5. Does the installed price include everything (installation, inverter, VAT)? See costs, VAT and quotes.
