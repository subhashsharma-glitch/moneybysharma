Why the gain on a gilt is free of CGT but the coupon is taxed

Gilts are taxed in two different ways: the coupon as income, the gain as an exempt capital gain. Here is the rule, the allowances and a worked example. Information, not financial advice.

Why the two are treated differently

Parliament exempted gilts from Capital Gains Tax: section 115 of the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992 says a gain on the disposal of gilt-edged securities “shall not be a chargeable gain”. HMRC’s manual says profits and losses on gilts are not charged under an income regime either, apart from gilt strips (SAIM2230 (opens in new tab), SAIM3040 (opens in new tab)). The coupon is different: income tax is charged on interest, and the interest on a gilt is a coupon. We found no mention of gilts in the Budget 2026 tax document we checked (Budget 2026 overview of tax legislation and rates (opens in new tab)), and section 115 on legislation.gov.uk was up to date to 24 November 2026.

That is why a low-coupon gilt bought below £100 can be tax-efficient: more of the return arrives as the exempt gain and less as taxable coupon. The same does not apply to gilt strips, which are taxed as income.

How the coupon is taxed

  • It is paid gross. The DMO’s taxation page says interest on registered gilts is payable without tax deducted (UK Debt Management Office – Taxation of gilts (page dated November 2016) (opens in new tab), a 2016 page, so check it is still current).
  • It counts as savings income. That is the same bucket as bank interest (GOV.UK – Personal Savings Allowance factsheet (archived 2018; lists income from government bonds as savings income) (opens in new tab)).
  • Personal Savings Allowance. £1,000 of savings interest is tax-free for basic-rate taxpayers, £500 for higher-rate taxpayers and £0 for additional-rate taxpayers. The starting rate for savings can also apply, up to £5,000 of interest, if your other taxable income is under £17,570 (GOV.UK – Tax on savings interest: how much is tax free (opens in new tab), checked 26 November 2026).
  • Reporting. Because no tax is taken off, you may need to tell HMRC about the coupon above your allowances, for example on a Self Assessment return.

Accrued Income Scheme

When gilts change hands, part of the price is accrued interest. The Accrued Income Scheme taxes the seller on interest built up to the sale and gives the buyer relief for interest they paid for. It does not apply if the total nominal value of all your securities was £5,000 or less at all times in the tax year and the previous one (GOV.UK/HMRC – HS343 Accrued Income Scheme (2025) (opens in new tab), checked 26 November 2026). Above £5,000 it applies to any transfer, however small. Gilts go ex-dividend 7 business days before each coupon date (HMRC Savings and Investment Manual SAIM4020 – cum div and ex div (gilts: 7 business days) (opens in new tab)).

The difference comes entirely from the £50 that is a gain rather than interest.

Rates from April 2027

At Budget 2026 the government announced that from 2027 to 2028 the savings income tax rates will be 24% (basic), 44% (higher) and 49% (additional), to be legislated in Finance Bill 2026-27 (GOV.UK/HM Treasury – Budget 2026: overview of tax legislation and rates (savings rates for 2027 to 2028) (opens in new tab), checked 26 November 2026). They are not law until that Bill passes. Gilt coupons are savings income, so they would be taxed at the new rates if enacted.
