What an EV charging calculator leaves out

A charging calculator answers one question: what does the electricity cost? It does not answer what the car costs to run. Here is what is missing, with sources. Information, not financial advice.

Try the numbers yourself

Put your own figures into the EV charging cost calculator. The values already in it are examples, so replace them with your own.

Open the ev charging cost calculator

Key points

  • The EV charging cost calculator multiplies units by prices. It ignores losses, changes in efficiency and everything that is not electricity.
  • Electric cars pay vehicle tax, and a pay-per-mile charge has been announced for April 2028 (not law yet).
  • Insurance, servicing and depreciation are not in the calculator either.

Charging losses

The calculator’s own note says charging losses are ignored. E.ON Next’s guide says some of the electricity you are billed for is lost as heat and in battery warming, from around 10% to 25% depending on the charger, cable and weather. The cost per mile article shows how an assumed 10% loss changes the sum, from 7.5p to about 8.4p per mile at 26.32p/kWh. That is one supplier’s guide, so treat the range as a rough guide.

Efficiency changes with conditions

The calculator uses one figure for miles per kWh. Which? says in its 2022 article that weather, driving style and speed all affect an electric car’s range, and that you get fewer miles on a motorway than around town. Its tests found real range averaged 18% below the official figure. A lower miles-per-kWh figure raises the cost per mile directly, since the sum is price ÷ miles per kWh. For example, 3.0 miles per kWh instead of 3.5 at 26.32p gives about 8.8p per mile rather than 7.5p. E.ON Next also notes that in cold weather more energy goes into warming the battery.

A home charge point

If you do not already have one, the Energy Saving Trust gives the typical cost of installing a home charge point as around £800 to £1,200 (page last updated 25 March 2025). It also says you need off-street parking, and typically a smart meter and a suitable tariff to get the most from it. The calculator’s home rate does not include this one-off cost.

Vehicle tax (VED)

According to GOV.UK’s rate tables, a zero-emission car pays £10 when it is first registered. From the second payment onward, electric cars pay the standard rate of £200 for 12 months. An electric car with a list price of more than £50,000 can pay an extra £440 a year for five years (from the second time it is taxed). Check GOV.UK’s table for which registration dates and prices are affected.

Pay-per-mile eVED (announced, not law yet)

GOV.UK’s page on Electric Vehicle Excise Duty (updated 28 October 2026) says that from 1 April 2028 a mileage-based charge will apply to electric cars: 3p per mile for battery-electric cars and 1.5p per mile for plug-in hybrids, rising with CPI from 2029 to 2030. It says primary legislation will be introduced, so it is not law until Parliament passes it, and the details could change.

Illustration, if it goes ahead as announced (hypothetical use). At 3p per mile, 8,000 miles a year would be 8,000 × 3p = £240 a year. That is 3p on top of the electricity cost per mile in the cost per mile article, for example 7.5p per mile at 26.32p/kWh and 3.5 miles per kWh. The eVED would be separate from, and in addition to, the cost of the electricity.

A fuller running-cost checklist

  • Electricity: your tariff and your mix of home and public charging (see home vs public and EV tariffs).
  • The efficiency you actually get, in the seasons and on the roads you actually drive.
  • A home charge point, if you need one.
  • Vehicle tax, and from April 2028 the announced pay-per-mile charge.
  • Insurance, servicing, tyres, depreciation and finance. The calculator includes none of these, and we give no figures here because they vary widely.
