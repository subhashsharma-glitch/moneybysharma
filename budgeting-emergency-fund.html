Budgeting and emergency funds

Start from what actually lands in your account, plan every pound, and build a cushion for the unexpected. Information only – not advice.

Information only – not advice. This page explains common budgeting ideas using official guidance from MoneyHelper and GOV.UK. It can't know your circumstances, and the worked numbers are illustrations, not targets.

Start with take-home pay

Plan with net pay – the amount that lands in your bank account – not your salary. On the example payslip (£30,000 salary, paid monthly, standard tax code, minimum workplace pension and a Plan 2 student loan) take-home is about £2,010.14 a month. Without a student loan it would be about £2,014.14, and on Plan 5 about £1,977.14. Your payslip will differ.

MoneyHelper's free Budget planner (opens in new tab) lets you list your income and outgoings, see what's essential and what you could save.

Sources (checked 17 October 2026): GOV.UK – Payslips: employee rights (opens in new tab); MoneyHelper – Budget planner (opens in new tab).

Four steps

  1. Start with take-home pay. Use the figure that arrives in your account.
  2. List the must-pays. Rent or mortgage, bills, phone, travel, debt repayments and subscriptions.
  3. Pay future you first. Set up a standing order to a savings account for just after payday, before you spend.
  4. Share out the rest. Turn what's left into a weekly spending amount and stick to it.

The 50/30/20 rule of thumb

A popular way to split take-home pay is 50% needs, 30% wants and 20% future (savings and extra debt repayments). It is a rule of thumb, not an official rule, and it isn't from a regulator – with high rent or low pay, many people can't hit it, and that's fine. Use it as a starting point and adjust.

Emergency funds: how much is enough?

An emergency fund is money set aside for the unexpected, such as a broken boiler, a car repair or a gap in income. MoneyHelper gives this rule of thumb: have three to six months' essential outgoings in an instant-access savings account. Essentials are things like rent or mortgage, food, heating, bills and anything else you can't live without.

Emergency fund targets for example monthly essentials
Monthly essentials3 months6 months
£1,000£3,000£6,000
£1,400£4,200£8,400

The £1,000 row is MoneyHelper's own example; £1,400 is an illustration. Your essentials are your own figure.

  • Any amount helps. Don't wait until you can hit the target: save what you can, as regularly as you can. MoneyHelper says saving smaller, regular amounts is often more effective than occasional large ones, and suggests a standing order or Direct Debit.
  • Priority debts first. MoneyHelper says it can be cheaper in the long run to pay off credit card debt, an unauthorised overdraft, payday loans, door-to-door lending or mortgage arrears before piling money into an emergency fund. See our priority debts section.
  • Keep it separate and accessible. An easy-access account means you can reach the money at once. See Savings for account types, and note the FSCS protects up to £120,000 per person, per banking licence ( details).
  • Then think about investing. MoneyHelper suggests building the emergency fund first; see risk, spreading and protection.

Sources (checked 17 October 2026): MoneyHelper – Emergency savings: how much is enough? (opens in new tab); MoneyHelper – What's the difference between saving and investing? (opens in new tab).

Plug the leaks

  • Forgotten subscriptions. Not used it this month? Cancel it and re-join later if you miss it.
  • Little and often. A £4 coffee five days a week is £4 × 5 × 52 = £1,040 a year (illustration).
  • The "it's only" trap. Delivery fees, in-app purchases and small buy now, pay later plans add up. BNPL is borrowing: see BNPL.
  • Lumpy yearly costs. Birthdays, Christmas, the MOT and annual insurance: divide the yearly total by 12 and save that amount monthly.

Eight common money mistakes

Common money mistakes and what to do instead
MistakeInstead
Budgeting with your salaryPlan with net pay. On the example payslip, £30,000 a year is about £2,010 a month after tax, National Insurance, pension and a Plan 2 loan. Yours will differ – see the payslip guide.
Opting out of the workplace pensionThink before you opt out: you usually lose your employer's contribution and the tax relief. See workplace pensions.
Paying only the credit card minimumPay in full by Direct Debit if you can, or as much as you can. See credit cards.
Never checking your tax codeCheck it each April and after changing job. See tax codes.
Savings sitting in a very low-interest accountCompare AER, access rules and FSCS protection; consider an ISA. See savings.
BNPL for everyday spendingTreat it as a loan. If you can't pay today, ask yourself whether you really will next month. See BNPL.
Moving money because a caller said soHang up and call 159 or use the number on your card. A genuine bank won't rush you. See scams and fraud.
Ignoring debt lettersOpen them. Free, confidential help is available – see free debt help.

These are general pointers, not advice for your situation.
