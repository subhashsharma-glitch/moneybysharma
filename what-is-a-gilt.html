What is a gilt, and how do low-coupon gilts work?

A gilt is a UK government bond. Here is what the terms mean and why a gilt with a small coupon can behave very differently from a savings account. Information, not financial advice.

The words you will meet

TermWhat it means
GiltA bond issued by the UK government through the Debt Management Office (DMO). The DMO publishes the list of gilts in issue (DMO – Gilts in issue (opens in new tab)).
Nominal valueThe face value the coupon and the final repayment are worked out on. Gilts are quoted per £100 nominal.
CouponThe fixed yearly interest, set when the gilt is issued and paid twice a year, as a percentage of nominal value.
Maturity dateThe date the government repays £100 per £100 nominal.
Clean priceThe quoted market price per £100 nominal, before accrued interest.
Accrued interestThe part of the next coupon that has built up since the last coupon date. A buyer normally pays it on top of the clean price (DMO – About gilts (opens in new tab), checked 26 November 2026).

This article is about conventional gilts. Index-linked gilts and gilt strips follow different rules and are not covered here.

How a gilt pays you

A conventional gilt pays its coupon every six months and repays £100 per £100 nominal on its maturity date. Gilts are traded on the market, so their price moves, mainly with interest rates. If you sell before maturity you could get back less than you paid; if you hold to maturity you receive £100 per £100 nominal, as set out in our gilts guide.

What “low-coupon” means

A low-coupon gilt pays a small yearly coupon. Because market interest rates are usually higher than that coupon, such a gilt tends to trade below £100. If you buy it below £100 and hold it to maturity, part of your return is the coupon and part is the rise from your purchase price up to £100. That rise is the point of interest to taxpayers, because it is treated differently from the coupon. See why the gain is exempt from CGT but the coupon is taxed.

A worked example (hypothetical)

Example only. This is not a real gilt or a real price. Assumptions: a gilt with a 1% coupon and exactly two years to maturity, bought just after a coupon payment (so no accrued interest) at a clean price of £95.00 per £100 nominal, £1,000 nominal, ignoring dealing costs.

ItemAmount
You pay (£1,000 nominal × £95 ÷ £100)£950
Coupons over two years (1% × £1,000 × 2)£20
Repaid at maturity£1,000
Total return before tax (£20 + £1,000 − £950)£70
Of which comes from the rise to £100£50

In this example £50 of the £70 is the rise to £100 and only £20 is coupon. The calculator above does the same sum for real gilts, using each gilt’s coupon and maturity date from DMO data and a price you enter.

What can go wrong

  • Price risk. If you sell before maturity the market price may be below what you paid.
  • Not a bank deposit. Gilts are government debt, not savings accounts, so they are not covered by the FSCS deposit limit (opens in new tab) that applies to bank and building society deposits.
  • Inflation. A conventional gilt’s payments are fixed in pounds, so rising prices reduce what they buy.
  • Costs. Brokers and platforms can charge for dealing and holding gilts.
