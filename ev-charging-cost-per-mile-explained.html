EV charging cost per mile, explained

Cost per mile is one division. The hard part is choosing the right two numbers. Here is how it works, and why your real figure may differ from a calculator’s. Information, not financial advice.

Try the numbers yourself

Put your own figures into the EV charging cost calculator. The values already in it are examples, so replace them with your own.

Open the ev charging cost calculator

Key points

  • Cost per mile = price per kWh ÷ miles per kWh.
  • The price you pay depends heavily on where and when you charge, so there is no single figure for an electric car.
  • Some of the electricity you pay for is lost as heat, so the miles per kWh on the car’s display can flatter the cost.

The sum

An electric car’s efficiency is measured in miles per kWh. The price of electricity is in pence per kWh. Divide one by the other:

Cost per mile = price per kWh ÷ miles per kWh.

That is the formula in the EV charging cost calculator. It uses separate prices for home and public charging, because they can be very different (see home vs public charging), and a share of charging done at home to blend them.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Assumptions – an example, not a quote. A car that does 3.5 miles per kWh (the calculator’s example value). Prices per kWh: 7p (a made-up overnight rate), 32.69p (Ofgem’s average electricity unit rate for 1 January to 31 March 2027, Direct Debit), 26.32p (Ofgem’s average for the previous quarter, 1 October to 31 December 2026), and 54p and 82p (Zapmap’s October 2026 average pay-as-you-go prices at public chargers, 3 to 49kW and 50kW and above).

So the same car can cost around 2p a mile or over 20p a mile, depending on the price. Zapmap’s own conversion for the two public averages is about 16p and 24p per mile. It uses an “average efficiency EV”, and the page does not say what figure it assumes. The Ofgem rates are national averages for default tariffs, and your own rate depends on your region, tariff and how you pay.

Charging losses

You are billed for the electricity that passes through the meter, but not all of it ends up in the battery. E.ON Next’s guide says energy is lost as heat in the car’s on-board charger and in cables, and in warming the battery. It says you might lose 10% on a warm day with a high-quality wallbox but 25% on a chilly night with a 3-pin plug. It also says the car’s display usually shows only the energy stored in the battery, so a smart meter or charger app will show a higher figure. This is one supplier’s guide, so treat the range as indicative.

Example with an assumed 10% loss (hypothetical). If the car does 3.5 miles per kWh stored in the battery, but 10% of the electricity from the meter is lost on the way, you get 3.5 × 0.90 = 3.15 miles per kWh drawn from the meter. At 32.69p that is about 10.4p per mile instead of 9.3p, and at 7p it is about 2.2p instead of 2.0p. The calculator ignores losses, so real home charging costs are a little higher than it shows.

Real-world efficiency

Miles per kWh is not fixed. Which? found in its 2022 tests that the real range of the electric cars it tested was on average 18% lower than the official figure. It says weather, driving style and speed all matter, and that you get fewer miles on a motorway than around town. That article is from 2022, so it is older than our other sources, but the point still holds: use the efficiency you actually get. Many cars show an average on the dashboard, and over a few weeks that is a better input than a brochure figure.

From a mile to a year

To get a yearly cost, multiply by your mileage. In the example above, 8,000 miles a year at 3.5 miles per kWh needs about 2,286 kWh of electricity a year (8,000 ÷ 3.5). At 32.69p that is about £747, and at the previous quarter’s 26.32p it would have been about £602. The calculator does this sum for a mix of home and public charging. For what it leaves out, see what the charging calculator leaves out.
