Students

Student finance for 2026/27, how repayments work, bank accounts, phones, travel, discounts and help if money gets tight. Information only – not advice.

Information only – not advice. This page explains how things work. It doesn't recommend any lender, bank, network or discount scheme, and it has no affiliate links. Figures are for England unless stated; always check the official source before you decide anything.

University funding (England, 2026/27)

Students from England apply to Student Finance England (SFE). There are two main loans, and you have to pay both back.

Loan2026 to 2027 maximum
Tuition Fee Loan, full-time courseUp to £9,790
Tuition Fee Loan, accelerated degreeUp to £11,750
Maintenance Loan, living with parentsUp to £9,118
Maintenance Loan, away from parents, outside LondonUp to £10,830
Maintenance Loan, away from parents, in LondonUp to £14,135
Maintenance Loan, year of a UK course studying abroadUp to £12,403
  • Tuition Fee Loan is paid straight to your university or college, which sets the fee.
  • Maintenance Loan is paid into your bank account at the start of each term. How much you get depends on where you live and your household income, so many students get less than the maximum. From 2026/27, care leavers' household income isn't used, so they can choose the maximum.
  • You reapply every year, and must update your account if your living arrangements change.
  • GOV.UK notes that student finance is changing for courses starting on or after 1 January 2027.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): GOV.UK – Student finance: new full-time students (2026 to 2027 amounts) (opens in new tab).

Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland

If you normally live elsewhere in the UK, you apply to your own nation's funding body, which has its own amounts, grants and rules. These differ from England's, so check there rather than using the figures above:

  • Wales: Student Finance Wales (opens in new tab)
  • Scotland: Student Awards Agency Scotland (SAAS) (opens in new tab)
  • Northern Ireland: Student Finance NI (opens in new tab)

Your loan's repayment plan also depends on where you're from and when you started. For example, GOV.UK says postgraduate students from Northern Ireland are on Plan 1 and those from Scotland are on Plan 4.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): Student Finance Wales (opens in new tab); Student Awards Agency Scotland (SAAS) (opens in new tab); Student Finance NI (opens in new tab); GOV.UK – Repaying your student loan: when it gets written off (opens in new tab).

Repaying: Plan 5

Students from England starting an undergraduate course on or after 1 August 2023 are on Plan 5. Repayments depend on income, not on how much you owe.

Plan 5 thresholds

  • £25,000 a year
  • £2,083 a month
  • £480 a week

How much and how long

  • You repay 9% of income over the threshold.
  • Interest is currently 4.1% and is added even when you aren't repaying.
  • Any balance left is written off 40 years after the April you were first due to repay.

Example (worked from the GOV.UK figures): pay of £2,500 a month is £417 over the £2,083 monthly threshold. 9% of £417 is about £37 a month.

For comparison, GOV.UK lists other plan thresholds as £26,900 (Plan 1), £29,385 (Plan 2), £33,795 (Plan 4) and £21,000 (Postgraduate Loan, repaid at 6%).

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): GOV.UK – Repaying your student loan: which repayment plan you're on (opens in new tab); GOV.UK – Repaying your student loan: how much you repay (opens in new tab); GOV.UK – Repaying your student loan: when it gets written off (opens in new tab); GOV.UK/SLC – How interest is calculated: Plan 5 (opens in new tab).

Repaying: Plan 2 (started 2012–2023)

Plan 2 is for students from England who started a course between 1 September 2012 and 31 July 2023, and students from Wales who started on or after 1 September 2012. Repayments depend on income, not on how much you owe.

Plan 2 thresholds

  • £29,385 a year
  • £2,448 a month
  • £565 a week

You repay 9% of income over the threshold.

How long and what changes

  • Any balance left is written off 30 years after the April you were first due to repay.
  • The threshold stays at £29,385 up to April 2030, then rises each year with the Retail Price Index (RPI), according to a Department for Education written answer. The announcements refer to English Plan 2 loans, so if you're from Wales, check with Student Finance Wales.

Interest on Plan 2

Plan 2 interest is a variable rate. While you study it is RPI plus 3%. After you leave your course it depends on your income:

Plan 2 interest rate by annual income (2026/27)
Annual incomeInterest rate
£29,385 or less4.1%
£29,386 to £52,8844.1% plus up to 1.9% (rising with income)
£52,885 or more6%

The 6% limit is described as "currently" in place. GOV.UK says the rate is usually RPI plus up to 3% but there is currently a cap of 6%. Treat the cap as something that could change and check the official page (opens in new tab) for today's rates. The Budget 2026 document (opens in new tab) (checked 28 October 2026) lists a 6% interest cap for Plan 2 and Plan 3 loans from 1 September 2026 to 31 August 2027, with no change to repayment thresholds.

Example (illustration, worked from the GOV.UK figures): pay of £3,000 a month is £552 over the £2,448 monthly threshold. 9% of £552 is £49.68, and GOV.UK's own examples round down, so about £49 a month is repaid through payroll.

Grants, bursaries and DSA

  • Bursaries, scholarships and awards. Each university or college sets its own rules on who qualifies, how much and how to apply. The money is paid to you directly and you don't usually pay it back. Ask your student support service.
  • Extra help. GOV.UK lists extra support if, for example, you have children, pay for childcare, have an adult who depends on you, or are under 25 and estranged from your parents. Some courses, such as nursing or social work, have their own funding.
  • Disabled Students' Allowance (DSA). Covers study-related costs because of a mental health problem, long-term illness, learning difficulty such as dyslexia, or other disability. It isn't means-tested and you don't pay it back. Undergraduates and postgraduates can get up to £27,783 a year. It can pay for specialist equipment, non-medical helpers, some taxi travel and other study support. You'll have a needs assessment first. If you're assessed as needing a new computer, you pay the first £200. Don't buy equipment before you're assessed – it won't be reimbursed.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): GOV.UK – Extra money from your university or college (bursaries and hardship funds) (opens in new tab); GOV.UK – Student finance: new full-time students (2026 to 2027 amounts) (opens in new tab); GOV.UK – Disabled Students' Allowance: what you'll get (opens in new tab).

Student bank accounts and overdrafts

Many banks offer a student current account. The main feature is usually an interest-free arranged overdraft – a limit you can borrow up to without paying interest while you study.

  • It's still borrowing. You have to pay it back. The limit is usually set by the bank, can be lower in your first year, and may depend on a credit check.
  • Stay inside the limit. Going past it can mean payments are refused. How you manage an overdraft shows on your credit file.
  • After graduation. Banks usually convert the account to a graduate account, and the interest-free limit tends to shrink over time. Once interest is charged, overdrafts can be expensive. Since 6 April 2020, FCA rules mean banks charge a single annual interest rate on overdrafts, with no fixed daily or monthly fees, and arranged and unarranged overdrafts are priced the same.
  • Compare the terms, not the freebies. Look at the interest-free limit in each year, how long it lasts after you graduate, and the rate charged afterwards.

See our bank accounts guide and credit scores guide for more.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): FCA – PS19/16: High-Cost Credit Review, overdrafts policy statement (rules in force 6 April 2020) (opens in new tab).

Mobile and broadband

  • SIM-only plans. If you already own a phone, a SIM-only plan pays for calls, texts and data only. They are often available on a 30-day rolling contract as well as 12 or 24 months.
  • Contract length versus term time. An academic year is roughly 9 to 10 months, and you may move each year. A 12, 18 or 24-month contract can outlast your tenancy, and leaving early usually means early-termination charges. A rolling contract gives more flexibility, often at a higher monthly price.
  • Halls Wi-Fi. Most halls include internet in the rent. Check this before you buy home broadband, and check coverage at your new address with Ofcom's checker (opens in new tab).
  • Switching. You can switch mobile provider and keep your number by texting your current provider (Text to Switch). Providers must also tell you when your contract is ending.

More detail on contracts, price rises and switching: Broadband & phones guide.

Student discounts

Three widely used schemes in the UK (listed alphabetically, not ranked):

  • Student Beans (opens in new tab) – an app and website where verified students get discount codes from retailers.
  • TOTUM (opens in new tab) – a student discount card (formerly the NUS Extra card), with free and paid options.
  • UNiDAYS (opens in new tab) – an app and website where verified students get retailer discounts.

A discount only saves money on something you were going to buy anyway. Many retailers also offer their own student discount directly.

Sources (checked 14 October 2026): Student Beans (official website) (opens in new tab); TOTUM (official website) (opens in new tab); UNiDAYS (official website) (opens in new tab).

Budgeting and hardship

  • Plan by term. Your Maintenance Loan arrives at the start of each term, so divide it by the number of weeks until the next payment.
  • Fixed costs first. Pay rent and essential bills first, then food and travel. What's left is for everything else.
  • Track your spending. Banking apps and simple spreadsheets can show where money is going.
  • Check you get everything you're entitled to. Bursaries and extra grants aren't always automatic.

For a step-by-step method and a cushion for surprises, see our budgeting and emergency funds guide.

Hardship funds. If you're struggling, your university or college may give you extra money from its hardship fund. GOV.UK lists examples of students who may qualify, such as student parents (especially single parents), mature students, students from low-income families, disabled students, care leavers and students who are homeless. Your university decides who qualifies and how much. The money is paid as a lump sum or in instalments and usually doesn't have to be paid back, though sometimes it's a loan. Contact your student services department.

If debts are building up, free help is listed on our debt help page.
