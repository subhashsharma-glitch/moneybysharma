Credit cards and deals

How credit cards work, what protects you, how to check the cost of a balance transfer, and an A–Z of card providers. Factual information only – we don't rate or recommend any card.

Information only – not advice. MoneyBySharma has no affiliation with, and receives no payment or commission from, any card provider or comparison site, and none of the links here are affiliate links. Nothing here is a recommendation or an invitation to apply. Offers depend on your circumstances and change often, so always check the current terms with the provider.
Borrowing costs money. Only borrow what you can afford to repay. Missing payments can lead to fees, more interest and damage to your credit record. If you're worried about debt, free and confidential help is available from MoneyHelper (opens in new tab), StepChange (opens in new tab) and National Debtline (opens in new tab).

Types of card

  • 0% balance transfer. You move debt from another card and pay no interest on it for an introductory period. There's usually a one-off fee, a percentage of the amount moved, and you often have to make the transfer within a set number of days.
  • 0% purchase. No interest on new spending for an introductory period. After that, the standard rate applies to whatever is still owed.
  • Money transfer. Sends money from the card into your bank account, usually for a fee. It's often treated differently from purchases.
  • Cashback and rewards. These pay back a percentage of your spending, or points such as supermarket points or Avios (airline points used by British Airways and others). The rewards are usually worth far less than the interest if you carry a balance.
  • Credit-builder. Lower limits and usually higher rates, for people with a short or poor credit history. Paying on time can help build a record.
  • Travel / no foreign-fee cards. These don't add a foreign transaction fee when you spend abroad. Cash withdrawals may still cost money.

How credit cards work

  • Representative APR. The APR includes interest and compulsory fees. "Representative" means the lender expects at least 51% of people accepted through that advert to get that rate or lower. Up to 49% may be offered a higher rate, a lower limit or a shorter 0% period.
  • Interest-free period on purchases. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, most cards charge no interest on purchases. This doesn't normally apply to cash withdrawals or balance transfers.
  • Minimum payments. Paying only the minimum keeps your account in order. But most of each payment can go on interest, so the debt takes much longer, and costs much more, to clear.
  • Cash withdrawals. Usually a cash fee and a higher interest rate that starts straight away, with no interest-free period.
  • Late and over-limit fees. Missing a payment or going over your limit can lead to a fee, losing a 0% deal and a mark on your credit file. The fee amounts are in each card's summary box.
  • Persistent debt rules (FCA, from 1 March 2018). If over 18 months you've paid more in interest, fees and charges than you've repaid of the borrowing itself, your card provider must contact you and suggest paying more if you can. If that's still the case after 36 months, the provider must offer ways to repay the balance in a reasonable time, usually 3 to 4 years. If you can't afford more, it must show forbearance, for example by reducing or waiving interest. The card may then be suspended.

Sources (checked 12 October 2026): FCA Handbook – CONC 3.5 Financial promotions about credit agreements (representative APR) (opens in new tab); FCA – Credit Card Market Study, Annex 2: how credit card products are regulated (section 75 range over £100 to £30,000; representative APR 51% test) (opens in new tab); FCA Handbook – CONC 6.7 persistent debt rules (CONC 6.7.27R onwards) (opens in new tab); FCA – PS18/4: Credit card market study, persistent debt and earlier intervention (rules from 1 March 2018) (opens in new tab).

Credit scores and eligibility checkers

  • Three credit reference agencies (CRAs): Equifax (opens in new tab), Experian (opens in new tab) and TransUnion (opens in new tab). Lenders may use one or more. You can get your statutory credit report free from each.
  • Soft search: used by eligibility checkers and for some quotes. Lenders can't see it, and it doesn't affect your credit score.
  • Hard search: made when you actually apply. Other lenders can see it, and several in a short time can make it harder to get credit.
  • Eligibility checkers show how likely you are to be accepted, using a soft search. They're not a guarantee, unless a card is described as "pre-approved".

Sources (checked 12 October 2026): ICO – Credit: your information and credit reference agencies (opens in new tab).

Protection: Section 75 and chargeback

  • Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974. If you pay at least partly by credit card for a single item or service costing over £100 and up to £30,000, the card provider is jointly liable with the seller if the seller breaches the contract or misrepresents the goods. That covers cases such as goods not arriving or the firm going bust. It applies even if you put only part of the cost on the card.
  • Chargeback. Chargeback isn't a legal right. It's a voluntary process under the card scheme rules (Visa, Mastercard and others) for asking your card provider to reverse a payment. It works on debit cards too and has no minimum amount, but there are strict time limits.
  • If your provider rejects a valid claim, you can complain to it and then to the Financial Ombudsman Service (opens in new tab).

Sources (checked 12 October 2026): legislation.gov.uk – Consumer Credit Act 1974, section 75 (opens in new tab); FCA – Credit Card Market Study, Annex 2: how credit card products are regulated (section 75 range over £100 to £30,000; representative APR 51% test) (opens in new tab).

Using cards safely

  • Pay in full by Direct Debit if you can. You won't miss a payment, and you won't pay interest on purchases.
  • If you can't pay in full, set the Direct Debit for more than the minimum.
  • Know when 0% ends and what rate applies afterwards. New spending on a balance transfer card may not be at 0%.
  • Do the balance transfer maths. A 3% fee on £3,000 is £90, paid up front. That's only worth it if you'd otherwise pay more than £90 in interest over the period.
