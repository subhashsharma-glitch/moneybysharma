Home vs public EV charging: cost per mile

Home charging and public charging are priced very differently. Here are the published prices, the VAT difference and what that does to a year’s charging bill. Information, not financial advice.

Try the numbers yourself

Put your own figures into the EV charging cost calculator. The values already in it are examples, so replace them with your own.

Open the ev charging cost calculator

Key points

  • Zapmap’s October 2026 averages for pay-as-you-go public charging are 54p/kWh (3 to 49kW) and 82p/kWh (50kW and above).
  • Ofgem’s average home electricity rate on default tariffs is 26.32p/kWh now, and 32.69p/kWh from 1 January 2027.
  • Electricity from public charge points is standard-rated for VAT (20%), while domestic electricity currently has a temporary zero rate until 30 June 2027.

Published prices

Public charging. Zapmap’s monthly index gives weighted average pay-as-you-go prices. For October 2026 they are 54p/kWh for standard and standard-plus chargers (3kW up to 49kW) and 82p/kWh for rapid and ultra-rapid chargers (50kW and above). Zapmap says the pay-as-you-go price is what drivers can pay without a membership or special deal, so many drivers pay less, and prices differ a lot between networks. It says its rapid and ultra-rapid average is up about 8% on a year earlier, and about 2% for the slower chargers.

Home charging. On a default tariff, Ofgem’s average Direct Debit electricity unit rate is 26.32p/kWh for 1 October to 31 December 2026 and 32.69p/kWh for 1 January to 31 March 2027, plus a standing charge. These are averages for England, Scotland and Wales. A time-of-use or EV tariff can have a much lower rate in some hours (see EV tariffs and off-peak charging). The Energy Saving Trust (page last updated March 2025) says charging at home is currently the cheapest way to charge, and that charging at home at off-peak times can save more.

The VAT difference

HMRC says electricity for domestic use is usually subject to the reduced rate of VAT, and a temporary zero rate has applied in Great Britain from 1 October 2026 (Ofgem says to 30 June 2027). Legislation that took effect on 1 November 2026 confirms that electricity supplied through publicly accessible charge points is not supplied for domestic use for VAT, so it is standard rated (20%). Electricity used to charge an EV as part of the general supply to your home may continue to qualify for the relief. HMRC’s brief is addressed to charge point operators. We have not checked whether each network’s advertised price includes VAT, so check this on the price you see.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Assumptions – an example, not a quote. 8,000 miles a year at 3.5 miles per kWh (about 2,286 kWh a year). Charging losses are ignored, as in the calculator. The home price is Ofgem’s default-tariff average, and the public price is Zapmap’s 82p/kWh rapid average. Your own mix and prices will differ.

Moving from 90% to 80% home charging adds about £127 a year in this example, and doing everything on rapid chargers costs about three times as much as doing everything at home on a default tariff. The table comes from the EV charging cost calculator’s own formula.

Why the mix matters

The share of charging you do at home matters at least as much as the exact prices. A driver who can charge at home most of the time has a very different cost profile from one who relies on public rapid chargers. Zapmap says typical EV drivers use the most expensive rapid chargers for only a fraction of the time.

If you cannot charge at home, the type of public charger matters: Zapmap’s two averages differ by 28p per kWh. Zapmap also notes that some operators offer promotional deals that make the price paid lower than the pay-as-you-go average.

Checking your own prices

  1. Home: look at the unit rate on your tariff, and whether you have a cheaper rate at night.
  2. Public: check the price per kWh in the charger network’s app before you plug in, and whether any extra fees apply.
  3. Put both into the calculator, with your own split between home and public.
  4. Remember the calculator ignores charging losses (see how cost per mile is worked out).
